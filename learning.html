Overview

Financial stability describes a state in which the financial system - including banks, markets, and financial infrastructure - can withstand shocks and continue performing its core functions without major disruption to the real economy. At its core, this means maintaining a system that can effectively channel savings into productive investment, allocate and price risk, facilitate payments, and support economic activity.

Financial stability does not imply that individual institutions never fail or that markets never experience volatility. Rather, it means that the financial system remains resilient enough to absorb disruptions and prevent problems from becoming widespread, undermining the provision of essential financial services or affecting the broader economy.

Financial instability can emerge when vulnerabilities build within the financial system and shocks are amplified through interconnected institutions, procyclical behaviour, and contagion channels. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for identifying and assessing risks that could threaten the stability of the financial system as a whole.

💡

Did You Know? At the time of its 2008 collapse, Lehman Brothers held over $600 billion in assets - still the largest corporate bankruptcy in U.S. history, and a textbook case of how contagion spreads through a highly interconnected financial system.

💡

Did You Know? The U.S. Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP), authorised in 2008, allowed the government to purchase up to $700 billion in distressed assets and inject capital directly into banks - an unprecedented level of direct government intervention in private financial institutions.

💡

Did You Know? The 2008 Global Financial Crisis was often compared with the Great Depression because both periods showed how problems within the financial system can amplify economic downturns. While the underlying causes differed, both crises highlighted the critical role of banks and financial markets in supporting economic activity - and the consequences when those systems become impaired.

💡

Did You Know? A bank can be financially healthy under normal conditions but still face severe stress if many depositors or investors lose confidence at the same time. This is why liquidity risk and confidence effects are central concerns in financial stability analysis.

💡

Did You Know? During the 2008 global financial crisis, a problem that began in the U.S. housing market spread rapidly across the global financial system because financial institutions were deeply connected through lending relationships, securities markets, and complex financial products. The crisis demonstrated that risks in one part of the financial system can quickly become risks for the system as a whole.

"The Broader Danger"

The risk that a disruption in one part of the financial system could spread or grow large enough to threaten the stability of the wider financial system and the broader economy.

The process by which distress spreads from one institution or market to others - through direct exposures, shared funding sources, or panic-driven behaviour.

"How Financial Behaviour Amplifies the Cycle"

The tendency for financial institutions and markets to take on more risk during economic upturns and pull back during downturns, making booms and recessions more extreme.

"How Risks Can Travel Through the System"

The network of financial relationships linking institutions, markets, and financial infrastructure, through which shocks or financial distress can spread from one part of the system to another.

Theme:Digital Innovation, Risk & Vulnerabilities

A stable and efficient payment system forms the foundation of a well-functioning financial system by enabling the safe, efficient, and reliable transfer and settlement of funds throughout the economy.

Payment systems are currently undergoing significant transformation, driven by innovations such as instant payment systems, distributed ledger technology (DLT), tokenisation, and central bank digital currencies (CBDCs). These innovations have the potential to enhance payment systems, while simultaneously introducing new risks, and policy challenges with implications for financial stability.

This section focuses on two key innovations - CBDCs and tokenisation - and explores their potential to reshape payment systems, including the opportunities they offer, the risks they introduce, and their implications for the broader financial system.

Key Topics

Why it matters

Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) is a digital form of central bank money issued as a liability of the central bank. Depending on its design, a CBDC may be available to the general public for everyday payments (retail CBDC) or used by financial institutions for wholesale settlement purposes (wholesale CBDC).

CBDCs have attracted significant attention due to a range of potential benefits, including improved payment efficiency, lower transaction costs, reduced frictions in cross-border payments, and continued access to central bank money. However, the benefits come with important trade-offs. While CBDCs could strengthen payment system resilience by providing a public digital alternative to commercial bank money, they may also affect financial intermediation. For example, if individuals shift significant holdings from commercial bank deposits to CBDCs, banks could face reduced deposit funding for lending, with potential implications for liquidity management, credit provision, and overall financial stability. CBDCs also raise challenges related to cybersecurity, operational resilience, and privacy.

Why it matters

Tokenisation is the process of representing an asset, claim, or form of money as a digital token that can be transferred and managed on a digital platform, often using distributed ledger technology (DLT).

Depending on its application, tokenisation can be applied to a wide range of financial instruments, including deposits and securities.

Tokenisation has attracted growing attention because of its potential to improve the efficiency of payments and financial transactions, streamline settlement processes, enhance transparency, and enable greater automation through programmable payments and smart contracts. It may also help reduce settlement risk and improve the speed and accessibility of cross-border payments. However, despite the potential benefits, the adoption of tokenisation raises questions about legal and regulatory frameworks, interoperability, cybersecurity, and operational resilience. As tokenisation becomes more widely adopted, managing these challenges will be essential to ensuring that innovation supports, rather than undermines, financial stability.

Overview

Macroprudential policy is the regulatory approach designed to safeguard the stability of the financial system as a whole by identifying and mitigating risks that could disrupt the provision of financial services and spill over to the broader economy. The approach gained prominence following the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, which demonstrated that ensuring the soundness of individual financial institutions alone was not sufficient to prevent system-wide instability.

Unlike microprudential policy, which focuses on the safety of individual institutions, macroprudential policy addresses risks that build across the financial system. It begins by identifying where systemic risks originate and how they evolve over time, before applying policy tools that strengthen the resilience of the financial system and reduce vulnerabilities. Together, macroprudential and microprudential policies provide a complementary framework for promoting a stable and resilient financial sector.

💡

Did You Know? The term "macroprudential" existed in obscure BIS discussions since the late 1970s — but the 2008 crisis turned it into the backbone of modern financial regulation, leading directly to new bodies like the European Systemic Risk Board and the U.S. Financial Stability Oversight Council, both established in 2010.

💡

Did You Know? Spain's central bank introduced "dynamic provisioning" — an early countercyclical capital buffer requiring banks to build up reserves during credit booms — as far back as 2000, years before such tools became standard worldwide after 2008.

💡

Did You Know? Before 2008, most banking regulation focused on making individual banks safer. The crisis revealed that even if each institution appears sound on its own, their collective behaviour can still create system-wide vulnerabilities.

💡

Did You Know? Many macroprudential tools are designed to "lean against the wind." Instead of responding after a financial boom turns into a crisis, they aim to moderate excessive risk-taking while the boom is still underway.

💡

Did You Know? The IMF, the BIS, and the Financial Stability Board (FSB)all play important roles in developing international guidance on macroprudential policy, but implementation is ultimately tailored by national authorities to reflect country-specific risks.

Building Blocks The Foundations of Macroprudential Policy

Systemic Risk: The Policy Objective

"Protecting the System"

Macroprudential policy aims to safeguard the stability of the financial system as a whole. Rather than focusing on the soundness of individual institutions, it seeks to reduce systemic risk and strengthen the financial system's ability to withstand and recover from shocks. Learn more about systemic risk in the Financial Sector Stability section.

Further Reading
  • Macroprudential Policy: An Organizing FrameworkPolicy Paper

    This IMF paper establishes systemic risk as the central focus of macroprudential policy and explains why a system-wide approach is essential for safeguarding financial stability. It presents a comprehensive framework for understanding how vulnerabilities are identified, monitored, and addressed through macroprudential policy.

  • Towards a macroprudential framework for financial supervision and regulation?Working Paper

    This seminal BIS paper explains why safeguarding financial stability requires looking beyond the health of individual institutions. It introduces the macroprudential approach, showing how interactions across the financial system can amplify shocks and why policy must address system-wide vulnerabilities rather than institution-specific risks.

The Policy Cycle

"Monitor, Assess, Respond"

Effective macroprudential policy is a continuous process. Policymakers monitor financial conditions, assess emerging vulnerabilities, implement appropriate measures, and review their effectiveness to ensure risks remain contained as the financial system evolves.

Further Reading
  • Macroprudential Policy: An Organizing FrameworkPolicy Paper

    Beyond defining the objectives of macroprudential policy, this IMF paper explains how policymakers move from identifying emerging vulnerabilities to selecting, implementing, and evaluating policy measures. It provides a practical framework for understanding macroprudential policy as a continuous cycle of monitoring, assessment, and response.

  • Operationalising the selection and application of macroprudential instruments (CGFS Papers No. 48)Report

    This BIS report examines how policymakers choose, calibrate, and evaluate macroprudential instruments. It highlights the practical challenges of translating risk assessments into effective policy actions and monitoring their impact over time.

Time Dimension

"When Do Risks Build?"

Financial vulnerabilities often accumulate gradually during periods of strong credit growth and favourable economic conditions. The time dimension focuses on identifying these build-ups early so that policy action can be taken before they threaten financial stability.

Further Reading
  • Systemic risk: how to deal with it?Policy Paper

    This BIS paper introduces the time dimension of systemic risk, explaining how financial vulnerabilities build gradually during credit booms and periods of strong economic growth. It shows why macroprudential policy seeks to act before these vulnerabilities become sources of financial instability.

  • Towards a Macroprudential Framework for Financial Supervision and RegulationWorking Paper

    This seminal BIS working paper explains why systemic risk has a strong time dimension, with vulnerabilities accumulating over the financial cycle before materialising during periods of stress. It provides the conceptual foundation for countercyclical macroprudential policy.

Cross-Sectional Dimension

"Where Is Risk Concentrated"

Systemic risk can arise when vulnerabilities are concentrated within particular institutions, markets, or sectors of the financial system. The cross-sectional dimension examines how risk is distributed across the financial system, helping policymakers identify where shocks could spread and where resilience may need to be strengthened.

Further Reading
  • Systemic risk: how to deal with it?Policy Paper

    This BIS paper explains the cross-sectional dimension of systemic risk, focusing on how vulnerabilities can become concentrated across institutions, markets, and sectors, increasing the potential for shocks to spread throughout the financial system.

  • Understanding Financial InterconnectednessPolicy Paper

    This IMF paper explores how linkages among financial institutions and markets can transmit and amplify shocks. It demonstrates why assessing the distribution of risk across the financial system is central to macroprudential surveillance.

Applications The Tools

Capital-Based Instruments

"Building Resilience"

Capital-based instruments aim to strengthen the ability of financial institutions to absorb losses and remain resilient during periods of stress. By requiring banks to hold additional capital during periods of rising vulnerabilities, these tools help reduce the likelihood that financial shocks could disrupt the wider financial system.

Examples

  • Countercyclical capital buffer
  • Capital conservation buffer
Further Reading
  • Basel III: A Global Regulatory Framework for More Resilient Banks and Banking SystemsReport

    This Basel Committee standard introduces the capital framework underpinning modern macroprudential regulation. It explains the purpose of capital buffers, including the Capital Conservation Buffer and the Countercyclical Capital Buffer, and how they strengthen banks' resilience during periods of financial stress.

  • Key Aspects of Macroprudential PolicyPolicy Paper

    This IMF paper explains how capital-based instruments help limit the build-up of systemic risk and enhance the resilience of the banking system. It discusses when these instruments should be activated, how they interact with other macroprudential tools, and the challenges involved in their implementation.

Borrower-Based Instruments

"Keeping Credit Sustainable"

Borrower-based instruments focus on limiting excessive borrowing and reducing vulnerabilities among households and businesses. By setting limits on lending practices, these tools help prevent the build-up of unsustainable debt that could amplify downturns and threaten financial stability.

Examples

  • Loan-to-value (LTV) caps
  • Debt-to-income (DTI) limits
Further Reading
  • Key Aspects of Macroprudential PolicyPolicy Paper

    This IMF policy paper explains how borrower-based instruments, such as loan-to-value (LTV) and debt-to-income (DTI) limits, help contain excessive credit growth and reduce the build-up of household and corporate vulnerabilities. It discusses when these measures are most effective and how they complement other macroprudential tools.

  • Operationalising the selection and application of macroprudential policy instrumentsPolicy Paper

    This BIS paper explores how policymakers determine when borrower-based instruments should be introduced, tightened, or relaxed. It discusses the practical considerations involved in calibrating these measures and evaluating their effectiveness in limiting systemic risk.

Liquidity-Based Instruments

"Preparing For Stress"

Liquidity-based instruments help ensure that financial institutions have sufficient liquid resources to meet their obligations during periods of market stress. By strengthening funding resilience, these tools reduce the risk that liquidity pressures could spread across the financial system.

Examples

  • Liquidity coverage ratio (LCR)
  • Net stable funding ratio (NSFR)
Further Reading
  • Basel III: The Liquidity Coverage Ratio and Liquidity Risk Monitoring ToolsPolicy Paper

    This Basel Committee standard introduces the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) and explains how it helps banks withstand short-term liquidity stress. It outlines the principles of liquidity risk management and the role of liquidity buffers in strengthening the resilience of the banking system.

  • Basel III: The Net Stable Funding RatioPolicy Paper

    This Basel Committee standard explains the Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR), which promotes more stable funding over longer time horizons. It shows how maintaining a sustainable funding structure reduces banks' vulnerability to funding disruptions and supports financial stability.

Institutional Framework

"Turning Policy Into Action"

An effective macroprudential framework requires clear responsibilities, coordination, and accountability among institutions. Central banks, financial stability committees, and regulatory authorities typically play key roles in identifying risks, selecting appropriate instruments, and ensuring that policies are implemented effectively.

Further Reading
  • Macroprudential Policy: An Organizing FrameworkPolicy Paper

    This IMF policy paper examines the institutional foundations of macroprudential policy, outlining the roles and responsibilities of central banks, financial regulators, and other authorities. It discusses the governance arrangements needed to support effective decision-making, coordination, and accountability in safeguarding financial stability.

  • Key Aspects of Macroprudential PolicyPolicy Paper

    This IMF policy paper explores how institutional arrangements influence the effectiveness of macroprudential policy. It examines issues such as governance, mandates, coordination among authorities, and the importance of assigning clear responsibilities for financial stability.

Overview

Data-driven decision-making (DDDM) is the practice of using evidence from data to guide organisational decisions rather than relying solely on instinct, habit, or anecdote. It does not replace professional judgement but instead provides a stronger evidence base for that judgement. In practice, it means routinely asking, "What does the data actually show?" before deciding what to do next and being willing to let the evidence influence the outcome.

Every organisation already generates data through sales transactions, customer interactions, operational processes, and financial reporting. DDDM turns that existing information into a consistent input for decision-making instead of allowing it to remain an underused by-product of day-to-day operations. When data becomes part of routine decision-making, organisations can identify patterns, evaluate performance, and respond to opportunities and risks with greater confidence.

Ultimately, data-driven decision-making is less about collecting more data and more about making better use of the data organisations already have.

💡

Did You Know? A widely cited MIT Sloan/Erik Brynjolfsson study of large firms found that organisations adopting data-driven decision-making practices measured meaningfully higher output and productivity than competitors relying primarily on intuition and experience.

💡

Did You Know? Surveys of executives consistently find that most organisations believe they are "data-driven" in principle, yet only a small minority report having actually embedded data into everyday decisions - the gap between collecting data and using it remains the norm, not the exception.

💡

Did You Know? Data has become one of the fastest-growing organisational assets. Organisations today generate vast amounts of data from routine activities — transactions, customer interactions, digital platforms, and operational systems. However, the value of data does not come from its volume; it comes from the ability to transform it into insights that improve decisions.

💡

Did You Know? More data does not automatically lead to better decisions. Having more information can improve decision-making, but only when organisations have the ability to identify relevant patterns, separate meaningful signals from noise, and interpret results correctly. Without analytical discipline, more data can create complexity rather than clarity.

💡

Did You Know? Data-driven organisations still rely heavily on human judgement. Even the most advanced analytics systems do not eliminate decision-making responsibility. Leaders must interpret results, understand context, consider trade-offs, and decide how evidence should influence action.

💡

Did You Know? Small organisations can benefit from data-driven decision-making without having large data teams. DDDM is not limited to large corporations with dedicated analytics departments. Even smaller organisations can gain insights by systematically analysing the information they already collect, such as customer behaviour, financial performance, and operational trends.

Value Creation Benefits of Data-Driven Decision-Making

Faster, More Confident Decisions

"Decide Better"

When relevant evidence is readily available, organisations can make decisions more efficiently and with greater confidence. Data provides a common reference point, reducing unnecessary debate and helping decision-makers explain and justify their choices.

Further Reading
  • Competing on Analytics: The New Science of Winning Book

Reduced Bias

"Test Assumptions"

Data provides a valuable check against cognitive biases that can influence judgement-based decisions, including overconfidence, recency bias, and sunk-cost thinking. By introducing objective evidence into the process, organisations can make decisions that are more balanced and consistent.

Further Reading
  • Thinking, Fast and SlowBook

    This book by Daniel Kahneman explores the two systems of thought that shape our decisions and explains how cognitive biases and mental shortcuts can influence judgement.

Better Resource Allocation

"Allocate Smarter"

Data helps organisations understand what drives performance by revealing patterns across products, customers, processes, and activities. These insights allow resources - such as time, capital, and effort - to be directed toward areas where they can generate the greatest value.

Further Reading

Earlier Warning of Problems

"Act Early"

Regular monitoring of key indicators allows organisations to identify emerging issues earlier, such as declining margins, changing customer behaviour, or operational weaknesses. Early visibility creates an opportunity to respond before problems become more difficult and costly to address.

Further Reading

Theme:Turning Data into Better Decisions

Organisations collect vast amounts of information, but data only creates value when it informs action.

Data-driven decision-making is about transforming existing information into meaningful evidence that supports planning, improves performance, identifies opportunities, and helps organisations make better strategic decisions with confidence.

Key Topics

Why it matters

Businesses need to understand not only what results they are achieving, but also how performance is changing, where gaps are emerging, and how outcomes compare with relevant objectives or benchmarks. Performance measurement helps provide this understanding by examining available data to assess how effectively and efficiently a business is performing.

Measures, such as productivity and efficiency analysis, trend analysis, benchmarking, and comparisons against targets, can be used to identify patterns in performance, assess progress over time, and highlight areas where performance differs across activities, periods or comparable businesses.

By transforming performance data into meaningful insight, performance measurement can help businesses move beyond simply tracking indicators. It provides a clearer understanding of where performance is strong, where improvement may be needed, and where resources or management attention may have the greatest impact.

Why it matters

Businesses are often required to make decisions where there is more than one possible course of action, competing priorities, and uncertainty about future outcomes. Decision analytics helps bring structure to complex decisions by examining the options available and the factors that may influence their outcomes.

Techniques, such as decision trees, scenario analysis and sensitivity analysis, can be used to compare alternatives, assess trade-offs, explore potential outcomes, and examine how uncertainty or changing assumptions may affect the preferred course of action.

By transforming available data into meaningful decision insight, decision analytics can help businesses move beyond relying solely on intuition or isolated indicators. It provides a clearer basis for evaluating options, understanding uncertainty and trade-offs, and making decisions that are better aligned with business objectives.
